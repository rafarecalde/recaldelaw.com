Cross-Border, International & Wealth Structuring

PPLI for Mexican Families, in Two Countries

By Rafael Recalde — November 22, 2026

Recalde Law Firm, P.A. is a Florida law firm with an office in Miami, Florida. We advise on United States law only. We do not practice Mexican law, and we do not give Mexican tax or succession advice. We do not practice the law of any other country. Formation of a non-U.S. entity, and the local law of that jurisdiction, are matters for local counsel. We handle the U.S. architecture: classification, the documents a U.S. custodian and a U.S. court will read, and coordination with the family's tax adviser.

Private placement life insurance is one contract that two tax systems may describe differently. Mexican families ask about it because the growth inside a life insurance contract, and the amount paid at death, can be treated differently in Mexico from a brokerage account that distributes dividends and gains. Whether this contract receives that treatment is a Mexican question. Mexican counsel gives the opinion. We do not. On the U.S. side the same contract is life insurance only if it meets the U.S. rules, and it is optional. A sound trust plan for a Mexican parent with U.S. children does not depend on a policy.

The U.S. mechanics of the wrapper, including sections 7702 and 817(h) and investor control, are already on the PPLI page and in PPLI in estate and income tax planning. This note is how the contract sits next to a foreign grantor trust and a later domestic trust. The audience is the RIA, the private banker, and the family office that will have to explain the policy to both counsels.

The U.S. conditions, in general

Section 7702 is what makes the contract life insurance for U.S. income tax. Cash value and the death benefit have to stay inside the corridor the section describes. A contract that fails that definition is not rescued by the carrier's cover page. Section 817(h) requires diversification inside a variable contract. Investor control is the separate limit on the family's hand in the account: if the owner directs the investments as if the account were a brokerage account, the U.S. tax adviser may treat the owner as owning those investments. The carrier, the investment manager, and the tax adviser design that relationship. Legal counsel reads who owns the contract and who the beneficiary is.

If the contract remains life insurance, section 101 generally excludes the amount paid by reason of the insured's death from the recipient's income. Section 101(a)(2) limits that exclusion when the contract was transferred for value, with exceptions that include a gift and a transfer to the insured. A pure ownership gift is a different file from a purchase of the contract, or of the company that holds it. The tax adviser checks the exceptions before anyone pays consideration. The same point appears in moving a policy from an LLC into an ILIT.

How the proceeds meet the trust

Paid to a trust by reason of death, proceeds that section 101 excludes are not income. They are not the distributable net income of the year, and they are not undistributed net income of a prior year. They sit in the trust as corpus. A later distribution of that corpus to a U.S. child is a different report from a distribution of accumulated foreign-trust income. The character still has to be written on the trustee's statement. "Capital" is the planning description. The return is the tax adviser's.

That is the U.S. reason families put the death benefit into the trust that will hold assets for U.S. children: the proceeds can arrive as an amount excluded under section 101, rather than as a dividend from a foreign company or as an accumulation distribution. The trust that receives them should be the trust the plan has already chosen for the period after death. In the design in a foreign grantor trust that becomes a Wyoming dynasty trust, that is the domestic trust, if the section 7701 tests are met at death. A policy does not itself make a foreign trust domestic.

Why the policy is optional on the U.S. estate-tax side

Section 2105(a) provides that insurance on the life of a nonresident who is not a U.S. citizen is not deemed property within the United States. For a Mexican parent who is that nonresident, the proceeds are generally outside the U.S. gross estate. The limited exemption under section 2102(b) is about U.S.-situs property. The policy is not what addresses U.S. real estate or stock of a domestic corporation. Those assets have their own situs, described in U.S.-situs assets inside a foreign trust.

Section 2042 is the inclusion rule when the insured is a U.S. person, or otherwise when the gross estate is a U.S. person's estate, and the insured holds incidents of ownership. A U.S. child who owns a policy on the child's own life is in that rule. An irrevocable life insurance trust is the ownership answer for that child, not a feature the parent's contract automatically has. The basic exclusion for a U.S. person is $15 million for 2026, indexed afterward. It is the child's figure if the child is the U.S. person. It is not the parent's.

What the adviser collects before the premium is paid

Mexican counsel's view of this contract, in writing, if the family is relying on a Mexican income-tax difference. The carrier's confirmation that the contract is being administered as life insurance for sections 7702 and 817(h), and that the family will not direct the account. The name of the owner during the parent's life, matched to the foreign grantor trust or to the person Mexican counsel requires. The name of the beneficiary at death, matched to the trust that should hold the proceeds. And a note that the trust plan stands if the policy is never issued. The policy is an addition. It is not the architecture.
