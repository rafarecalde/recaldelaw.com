Life Insurance and the $15 Million Exemption

By Rafael Recalde — November 22, 2026

Recalde Law Firm, P.A. is a Florida law firm with an office in Miami, Florida. We advise on United States law. What follows is general. It describes no family's facts and no family's numbers. Tax results are confirmed with the family's tax adviser. We advise on the legal structure.

A term policy or a permanent policy is easy to leave off the mental balance sheet. The premium is modest, the cash value may be small, and the family thinks of the contract as something that pays the children. For federal estate tax, the figure that counts is the amount payable at death, when the insured still holds incidents of ownership. For 2026 the basic exclusion amount is $15 million per person, and it is indexed afterward. A death benefit that looked like family protection can be the amount that carries a Florida estate across that line.

The exclusion is per person. A married couple may have two. A surviving spouse may also be able to use a deceased spouse's unused exclusion, if the estate files the return that makes that election. The tax adviser adds those figures. This note is about the one asset families forget to add: the insurance.

Why the death benefit is in the estate

Section 2042 includes life insurance proceeds in the gross estate in two situations. Proceeds receivable by the estate are included. Proceeds receivable by other beneficiaries are included when the decedent possessed, at death, any incident of ownership, exercisable alone or with another person.

Incidents of ownership are the economic powers over the contract. They include the power to change the beneficiary, to surrender or cancel the policy, to assign it, to revoke an assignment, to pledge it, or to borrow against the cash value. Holding those powers matters. The size of the cash value does not decide the estate-tax amount. The estate-tax amount is the proceeds. A policy with a small cash value and a large death benefit is still a large inclusion if the insured can change the beneficiary the week before death.

Naming a trust as beneficiary, while the insured still owns the contract, leaves the incidents with the insured. Section 2042 still counts the proceeds. The beneficiary designation and the ownership have to match. A designation of the estate brings the proceeds in under the first rule, and it also sends the money through probate.

What an ILIT changes

An irrevocable life insurance trust is a trust that owns the contract. The trustee, who is not the insured, holds the incidents. The insured does not serve as trustee of that trust and does not keep a power to change the beneficiary, to borrow, or to surrender. If that design holds, the proceeds are not included under section 2042. The trust receives them and applies them under the instrument: held for the family, or loaned to the estate so the estate can pay tax and expenses, if the instrument allows a real loan.

Two paths lead there. The trustee can apply for a new policy, so the insured never owns it. Or the insured can transfer a policy he or she already owns. A transfer of a policy the insured owned is a transfer that section 2035 can pull back into the estate if the insured dies within three years. The amount brought back is the proceeds, the amount section 2042 would have included. The three-year rule is discussed with the mechanics of a transfer in moving a policy from an LLC into an ILIT. A policy the trustee buys from the start is a different acquisition: there is no transfer of a policy the insured held.

The gift-tax value of a policy given away during life is the regulatory value under Treasury Regulation 25.2512-6. That value may differ from both the cash surrender value and the death benefit, and the difference is especially wide for a private placement policy. The tax adviser is the one who puts a number on the gift-tax return. Premiums the insured pays later are further gifts, which is the subject of Crummey letters.

Where this sits next to the rest of the plan

An ILIT is a single-purpose trust. The reasons for keeping the policy out of a broader irrevocable trust are in why a policy gets its own trust. Families who use private placement life insurance as an investment wrapper already have a longer treatment on this site, including sections 7702 and 817(h) and investor control. Those questions stay on the PPLI page and in PPLI in estate and income tax planning. This note is the inclusion question for a U.S. family, in Florida or elsewhere, counting a contract it already owns against a $15 million exclusion.
