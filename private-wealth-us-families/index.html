Private wealth counsel for wealthy U.S. families and business owners.

For wealthy U.S. families — business owners, families after a liquidity event, and finance professionals — we design the legal structure around a business sale and around moving growth to children and later generations while that growth is still ahead. The work includes grantor retained annuity trusts, sales to grantor trusts, dynasty trusts, spousal lifetime access trusts, family limited partnerships and LLCs, and life insurance trusts, read against the federal estate, gift, and generation-skipping transfer tax. For wealthy U.S. families

The family is our client. A CPA, wealth adviser, or trust company may sit in the same conversations. They keep their own work. We advise the family on the legal structure — the instruments, the holding company, and the title — and we coordinate with them on what each piece does. The figures below are for 2026. The exclusion amounts are adjusted for inflation in later years. Outcomes depend on the family's facts and on the return the tax adviser signs.

Federal estate, gift, and generation-skipping transfer tax

For a U.S. citizen or a person domiciled in the United States, gift tax and estate tax share one basic exclusion amount. Public Law 119-21 amended Section 2010(c)(3) and set that amount at $15 million per individual for gifts made in 2026, and for the estate of a person who dies in 2026. Rev. Proc. 2025-32 confirms the $15 million figure. It also confirms that the generation-skipping transfer exemption under Section 2631(c) is $15 million for 2026, and that both amounts are adjusted for inflation for later years. The same revenue procedure sets the Section 2503(b) annual exclusion for 2026 at $19,000 per donee, and only for a present interest.

A gift in trust is often a future interest, so the annual exclusion does not apply unless the instrument creates a present-interest right that actually works. Gift-splitting under Section 2513 lets a married couple treat a gift by one spouse as made half by each. It is available only if both spouses are U.S. citizens or residents at the time of the gift, and both spouses consent on the gift-tax return. It is not available when either spouse is a non-resident alien. Once made, the election covers all gifts either spouse makes to third parties during that year while married. A surviving spouse may be able to use a deceased spouse's unused exclusion if the estate files the return that makes that election. The tax adviser adds those figures. The timeline for a gift before a sale is in gifting ahead of a liquidity event and in rollover equity before the valuation step-up.

Dynasty trusts and GST allocation

A dynasty trust is an irrevocable trust that can run for children and for the generations after them. The generation-skipping transfer exemption described above is what lets a transfer to grandchildren, or to a trust for them, pass without the generation-skipping transfer tax to the extent of the exemption allocated to it. The allocation is made on the gift-tax return. Some lifetime transfers to a trust for grandchildren receive an automatic allocation unless the donor elects out on the return, so the return should state the allocation either way.

Duration is a Florida question for a family signing here. Under Section 689.225, Florida Statutes, a trust created on or after July 1, 2022 may use 1,000 years in place of the 90-year period, unless the instrument requires an earlier end. A trust created after 2000 and before July 1, 2022 may use 360 years. A family signing a new trust in 2026 can have a long Florida trust. Duration, by itself, is a weak reason to choose another state's trust code. A family that lives in Florida and asks about a trust governed by Wyoming law is the example on this site, in a Wyoming irrevocable trust when you live in Florida. Investment and distribution roles can be divided under Florida's directed-trust statute. That division is in directed trusts.

Spousal lifetime access trusts

A spousal lifetime access trust is an irrevocable trust one spouse creates for the other. The donor makes a completed gift. The gift uses the donor's basic exclusion to the extent it exceeds any annual exclusion that actually applies. Gift-splitting is generally not available for a gift to a SLAT when the spouse who would consent is a beneficiary. The narrow exception is when the spouse's interest can be valued separately from the interests of the other beneficiaries. If the transfer is respected, later growth is outside the donor's estate. The beneficiary spouse can receive distributions under the standard the instrument states. That access belongs to the beneficiary spouse. The donor is not a beneficiary.

Sections 2036 and 2038 include property the donor transferred and still has a string on: the right to the income or the use of the property, the right to say who enjoys it, or the power to alter, amend, revoke, or terminate the transfer. The donor does not serve as the trustee who decides distributions to the spouse. If the spouse is a trustee, distributions to the spouse are usually limited to health, education, maintenance, and support, so the spouse does not hold a general power of appointment under Section 2041. While the spouse can receive income, Section 677 often treats the donor as the owner of the trust for income tax. The donor reports the income and pays the tax. The trust can still be a completed gift if the estate-tax strings are absent. If the beneficiary spouse dies first, the access ends. A provision that sends the property back to the donor at that death is a different estate-tax result from the one the gift was made to achieve. If each spouse creates a trust for the other, interrelated trusts that leave each spouse in about the same economic position can be treated as if each spouse had created a trust for himself or herself, with estate inclusion under Sections 2036 and 2038. The Code states no safe harbor, and there is no statutory waiting period. A general power of appointment under Section 2041 would include the appointed property in that spouse's estate, so it is not a useful way to make the trusts differ. Timing, assets and values, the distribution standard, and the powers of appointment are discussed in spousal lifetime access trusts and in two SLATs and the reciprocal trust doctrine.

Grantor retained annuity trusts

A grantor retained annuity trust under Section 2702 pays the donor a qualified annuity. Appreciation above the Section 7520 hurdle can pass to the remainder for a small taxable gift. The rate that matters is the Section 7520 rate for the month the trust is funded. If the donor dies during the term, all or most of the trust property is generally included in the estate: the amount needed to produce the remaining annuity at the Section 7520 rate, up to the full value. The estate-tax inclusion period makes a GRAT a poor way to move generation-skipping transfer exemption to grandchildren at the outset. The technique assumes a U.S. person who can be expected to survive the term.

Around a business sale, a GRAT is an alternative, not the default. It is a poor fit when a sale may close inside an annuity term that was not built around a live deal. A sale to a grantor trust shifts the mortality risk into the note: if the sale holds, the property is already out, and what comes back if the grantor dies is the unpaid note. Both are discussed, as choices next to an outright gift, in rollover equity before the valuation step-up and in selling to an intentionally defective grantor trust versus gifting.

Life insurance trusts and Crummey powers

Section 2042 includes life insurance proceeds in the gross estate in two situations. Proceeds receivable by the estate are included. Proceeds receivable by other beneficiaries are included when the decedent possessed, at death, any incident of ownership, exercisable alone or with another person. Those incidents include the power to change the beneficiary, to surrender or cancel the policy, to assign it, to revoke an assignment, to pledge it, or to borrow against the cash value. Naming a trust as beneficiary, while the insured still owns the contract, leaves the incidents with the insured. The estate-tax amount is the proceeds, not the cash value. For a family counting a large death benefit against the basic exclusion, that arithmetic is in life insurance and the $15 million exemption.

An irrevocable life insurance trust owns the contract. The trustee, who is not the insured, holds the incidents. The insured does not serve as trustee of that trust. A policy the trustee applies for, which the insured never owns, is a different acquisition from a policy the insured transfers. A transfer of a policy the insured owned is a transfer that Section 2035 can pull back into the estate if the insured dies within three years. The amount brought back is the proceeds. The gift-tax value of a policy given during life is the regulatory value under Treasury Regulation 25.2512-6. That value may differ from both the cash surrender value and the death benefit. The tax adviser puts the number on the return. Premiums paid later are further gifts. See why a life insurance policy gets its own trust and moving a PPLI policy from an LLC into an ILIT. Investor control, Section 7702, and Section 817(h) stay on private placement life insurance and in PPLI in estate and income tax planning.

The annual exclusion applies to a present interest. A gift in trust is a future interest unless someone has a present right to take the property. The withdrawal right, and the letter that tells the beneficiary about it, is how a premium gift is built to qualify for the $19,000 annual exclusion. The instrument sets the number of days. Thirty days is a common period in instruments. It is a drafting choice. The Code does not fix the number of days. When the beneficiary is a minor, notice usually goes to the minor's parent or guardian, or to another adult the instrument names. Many instruments avoid naming the donor. If the beneficiary does not withdraw, the right lapses. Section 2514(e) treats a lapse of a general power as a release to the extent the property exceeds the greater of $5,000 or 5 percent of the aggregate value of the assets out of which the lapsed power could have been satisfied. The notices are explained in Crummey letters in plain English.

Sales to an intentionally defective grantor trust

In a sale, the trust buys the asset and the grantor receives a promissory note. If the sale is for full value, the gift is limited to whatever was transferred for less than value — commonly a seed gift so the trust has assets of its own before it owes the grantor, and any difference between the sale price and fair market value. There is no statutory seed percentage. A convention in practice, and only a convention, is to fund the trust with a gift on the order of ten percent of the intended sale price so the note is not the trust's entire capital. Some structures add a guarantee. Neither the convention nor a guarantee is a safe harbor.

The note has to bear adequate interest. For a debt instrument issued in a sale, Section 1274 looks to the applicable federal rate, which the IRS publishes monthly and which depends on the term: short-term, mid-term, or long-term. For gift tax, a note bearing at least the applicable federal rate for its term is the accepted floor. A lower rate can make part of the sale a gift. The rate that matters is the rate for the month of the sale and for the term of the note that is actually signed. If the sale is respected, the note stays in the grantor's estate and appreciation above that rate accrues to the trust. While grantor status continues, the sale is generally ignored for income tax. Rev. Rul. 85-13 is the standard citation: the grantor and the grantor trust are not separate taxpayers for this purpose, so the grantor does not recognize gain on the sale, and interest on the note is not separately taxed as if a third party had paid it. The note still has to be paid. Forgiveness of the note is generally a gift. During the term, the grantor reports the trust's income. That tax payment reduces the grantor's estate without a further gift, within the lines of Rev. Rul. 2004-64. Many instruments let an independent trustee reimburse the grantor for that income tax. Under Rev. Rul. 2004-64 and Florida law, that power alone does not pull the trust into the grantor's estate.

Grantor status ends when the power that caused it is released, when the instrument says it ends, or at death. If status ends while the note is still outstanding, whether gain is recognized is fact-specific. The sold property, if the sale was respected and no Section 2036 string remains, generally is not in the estate. Basis in that property stays a carryover basis. The family does not receive a Section 1014 adjustment in an asset that is outside the estate. Rev. Rul. 2023-2 confirms there is no basis adjustment for an asset in a grantor trust that is not in the estate. A substitution power that can bring a chosen asset back is covered in step-up in basis and the swap power. Section 672(f) generally prevents a person who is not a U.S. citizen or resident from being treated as the owner of a trust except in limited cases. This sale is a U.S.-grantor structure. The other case is on Private Wealth for international families. The comparison with an outright gift is in selling to an intentionally defective grantor trust versus gifting.

Family limited partnerships and LLCs

A family limited partnership or a family LLC is a holding company. It is how a business owner transfers interests in a closely held business without handing a child the checkbook. A general partner, or a manager, manages. The other owners hold the economics. A completed gift is a gift of that interest to a trust or to a child. Later appreciation accrues to the person who received it. If the transfer is respected, only what the donor kept stays in the estate. The interest is valued as what it is: a non-controlling, often illiquid stake, not a proportionate slice of the underlying assets at a control price. The size of any difference is an appraisal. It is not a standard percentage.

Section 2036 includes property the donor transferred and still enjoys, or as to which the donor kept the right to say who enjoys it. Section 2038 reaches a retained power to alter, amend, revoke, or terminate. An entity that pays the donor's personal expenses, or that the donor can liquidate at will with the family, is a different fact pattern from an entity with its own accounts and a manager who manages. Section 2703 provides that value is determined without regard to an option, agreement, or restriction, unless the arrangement is a bona fide business arrangement, is not a device to transfer property to the family for less than full consideration, and is comparable to arm's-length deals. Section 2704 disregards certain applicable restrictions on liquidation that are more restrictive than the default rule under state law and that the family can lift. Section 2701 is the recapitalization trap: a donor who gives the junior interest to family and keeps a preferred or other applicable retained interest that is not a qualified payment can have that retained interest valued at zero, so the gift is larger than the model showed. Adequate disclosure on the gift-tax return is what starts the limitations period. The operating note is family limited partnerships and trust-owned LPs. A gift of units in a rental company to a SLAT, as distinct from a deed of a residence, is in gifting a vacation-rental LLC to a SLAT.

Florida homestead

Florida homestead is a state-law status. It is not the federal basic exclusion, and it does not decide whether the home is in the federal gross estate. It matters to a young family because of the spouse and because of minor children. Article X, section 4(c) of the Florida Constitution provides that the homestead shall not be subject to devise if the owner is survived by a spouse or a minor child, except that the homestead may be devised to the owner's spouse if there is no minor child. Section 732.4015, Florida Statutes, states the same limit. For that purpose the owner includes the grantor of a trust described in Section 733.707(3) that exists at death, and a disposition of the homestead by that trust is a devise. A revocable trust's gift of the house to the surviving spouse does not control when a minor child also survives.

Section 732.401(1) provides that if a spouse and one or more descendants survive, the spouse takes a life estate and the descendants living at the death take a vested remainder, per stirpes. Section 732.401(2) lets the surviving spouse elect, instead, an undivided one-half interest as a tenant in common, within six months after the death and during the spouse's lifetime. Section 732.401(5) provides that this descent does not apply to property the decedent owned as tenants by the entireties or in joint tenancy with right of survivorship. The same constitutional section provides that the owner of homestead real estate, joined by the spouse if married, may alienate the homestead by mortgage, sale, or gift. Section 689.111 provides that joinder may be accomplished through a power of attorney executed in the manner of a deed. It does not dispense with joinder. Section 732.4017 provides that a lifetime transfer of homestead, including a transfer to an irrevocable trust, is not a devise subject to these limits, and does not descend under Section 732.401, if the owner keeps no power, in any capacity and alone or with anyone else, to revoke or take back the interest transferred. The spouse still has to join in the deed. A deed to the trustee of a fully revocable trust, where the owner can take the property back, is not that transfer. A spouse may be able to waive homestead rights in the manner Florida law allows. A minor child cannot. The two notes are Florida homestead and transfers into a trust and Florida homestead, a spouse, and minor children.

Florida community property trusts

Florida is a common-law property state. Title and the source of funds usually decide which spouse owns an asset. At the first death, the decedent's interest can receive a new basis under Section 1014. The survivor's separate interest keeps its old basis. A community property trust is the Florida statute that lets spouses opt chosen assets into community property, so that the basis rule for community property can apply to both halves. The Community Property Trust Act is the part of Chapter 736 that begins at Section 736.1501. It applies to a trust created, amended, restated, or modified on or after July 1, 2021.

Section 736.1503 sets four requirements. The trust expressly declares that it is a community property trust. At least one trustee is a qualified trustee. Both spouses sign it, with the formalities required for a Florida trust. At the beginning, in capital letters, it contains substantially the notice the statute sets out. A qualified trustee, under Section 736.1502, is a natural person who resides in Florida, or a company authorized to act as a trustee in Florida. Section 736.1511 provides that, for purposes of applying Section 1014(b)(6), a community property trust is considered a trust established under the community property laws of Florida. Section 1014(b)(6) treats the surviving spouse's one-half share of community property as property acquired from the decedent if at least one-half of the community interest was includible in the decedent's gross estate. The decedent's half is in the estate and receives a new basis. The survivor's half is not in the decedent's estate, but Section 1014(b)(6) treats it as acquired from the decedent, so it also receives a new basis. The IRS has not ruled on whether a community property trust that spouses elect into under a common-law state's statute qualifies, and older federal authority treated elective community property regimes differently from mandatory ones. The result is not certain. The federal computation is the tax adviser's. The community property trust agreement decides whether the trust is revocable. Unless it says the trust is irrevocable, the spouses may amend or revoke it, and after the first death the survivor may amend it as to the survivor's half (Section 736.1504). Florida's general rule for a revocable trust holding community property lets either spouse revoke alone but requires both to amend (Section 736.0602(2)). A joint revocable trust that does not meet Section 736.1503 does not produce the Section 1014(b)(6) adjustment, and titling an account in both names does not either. See Florida community property trusts and basis at the first death and amending or restating a revocable trust.

Charitable gifts

Some families also give through a charitable remainder trust, a charitable lead trust, a donor-advised fund, or a private foundation, often beside a sale or a transfer to children. This site does not have a separate note on those vehicles. We do not restate deduction limits or payout formulas here. The tax treatment is the CPA's computation. When a charitable gift is part of the same structure as the business or the trusts for the children, we coordinate the legal documents with the family and with the charity's counsel.

Asset protection

The structure is there so ownership continues and so personal risk is not the same thing as family capital. The ordinary tools are a spendthrift provision, a trustee who administers, and an entity that keeps its own accounts and does not pay personal expenses. A transfer the donor can still enjoy is still read under Sections 2036 and 2038 for the federal estate tax. A transfer made when a creditor's claim is already live is a question for litigation counsel as well as for the tax adviser. Choosing another state's trust statute does not, by itself, decide how a Florida court will treat a transfer the person can still benefit from. The example on this site is a Wyoming qualified spendthrift trust for a family that lives in Florida, described in a Wyoming irrevocable trust when you live in Florida. A private trust company, when the family wants one, is a separate question, in a Wyoming private trust company for a Florida family.

Around a sale, and for children

The usual moment is a business the family still owns, or a sale or recapitalization that will reset the value. A transfer before that price is fixed is a different gift from a transfer after it. The holding company, the trust that receives the interest, and the note or the annuity, if one is used, have to be signed before the economics move. Appraisal, disclosure on the gift-tax return, and who reports the income are part of the same file. The tax adviser models the figures. We draft the structure those figures assume.

Children change the documents. A minor beneficiary of a life-insurance trust needs an adult who can receive the withdrawal notice, and the instrument should say who that is. A homestead with a minor child in the family is not disposed of by a sentence in a revocable trust that leaves the house to the spouse. A trust that is meant to run past the children's lives needs generation-skipping transfer exemption allocated to it, confirmed on the gift-tax return, not only a long perpetuities period in the instrument. None of that is a form. It is the family's facts, read against the statutes above.
