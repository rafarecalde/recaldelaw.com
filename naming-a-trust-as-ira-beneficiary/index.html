Naming a Trust as IRA or 401(k) Beneficiary

By Rafael Recalde — November 22, 2026

Recalde Law Firm, P.A. is a Florida law firm with an office in Miami, Florida. We advise on United States law. What follows is general. It describes no family's facts and no family's numbers. Tax results are confirmed with the family's tax adviser. We advise on the legal structure.

An IRA or a 401(k) can name a trust as beneficiary. The custodian then asks whether it may look through the trust and treat the trust's beneficiaries as the designated beneficiaries. The payout rules themselves, including the ten-year rule and the eligible designated beneficiaries, are in the stretch IRA and the 10-year rule. This note is about the trust.

Looking through the trust

The regulations under section 401(a)(9) look through a trust when four things are true. The trust is valid under state law. It became irrevocable at the owner's death. The beneficiaries who count are identifiable from the instrument. The trustee gives the custodian the documentation the regulations require, on the schedule those regulations set, which falls in the year after the death. The tax adviser and the custodian work from the current deadline. A trust that fails those conditions is not looked through, and the payout is the one that applies when there is no designated beneficiary.

The trust has to be named on the beneficiary form. A will that pours the estate into a trust does not name the trust as beneficiary of the retirement account. If the form says "Estate," the account is payable to the estate. The pour-over may bring the proceeds to the trust later, after probate, on the estate's payout schedule. The form controls.

A conduit trust

In a conduit trust, the trustee's duty is to pay out to the beneficiary, as the retirement distributions arrive, the amounts the trust receives from the account. The beneficiary who must receive them is the person who counts. The payout period follows that person's status under section 401(a)(9). If that person is an eligible designated beneficiary, the life-expectancy options may be available. If not, the ten-year rule applies, and the money still leaves the trust as it leaves the account.

The income tax follows the money to the beneficiary. The trust does not accumulate the retirement distribution. A conduit trust is a poor fit when the reason for using a trust was to keep a large check out of a beneficiary's hands at a young age. The retirement money arrives on the retirement calendar, not on the trust's ordinary distribution standard.

An accumulation trust

In an accumulation trust, the trustee may hold the retirement distribution and distribute it later under the trust's ordinary standard. The people who can receive under the instrument are the ones who count. A charity, the estate, or another non-person inside that group can cost the trust its look-through treatment. Which contingent beneficiaries the regulations disregard is a reading the tax adviser does against the instrument. It is not a conclusion to draw from the word "accumulation" on the cover.

After the SECURE Act, an accumulation trust for beneficiaries who are not eligible designated beneficiaries is still on the ten-year rule. The trust can hold the distributions inside the ten years. It cannot stretch them over a grandchild's life. Income retained in the trust is taxed to the trust. Trust income-tax brackets are compressed. A large IRA distribution retained in the trust can cost more income tax than the same distribution paid out to a beneficiary. The tax adviser runs that comparison before the form is signed.

A conduit trust fits when a chosen person should receive the retirement money as it comes out and the family wants one countable beneficiary. An accumulation trust fits when the family wants a spendthrift structure, or a pot for several people, and accepts the ten-year outside date and the income tax of retaining the distribution. Either trust should be coordinated with the revocable trust's beneficiary provisions, covered in amending or restating a revocable trust. Amending the revocable trust does not amend the beneficiary form.

Write as the family, or as the adviser.
