Fund Formation & Cross-Border Structuring

Open-Ended vs. Closed-Ended Cayman Funds: Start With the Exit Rights

By Rafael Recalde — November 7, 2026

In this series. Exit rights are the starting question. Liquidity terms cover lock-ups, gates, suspensions, and payment timing. Feeder and parallel funds cover where the money is invested. Board, GP, manager, and administrator covers who does what. The practice page is Cayman funds.

A sponsor planning a cross-border fund often starts with an entity question: should the vehicle be a Cayman company or a Cayman limited partnership? The more useful first question is what investors will be entitled to do with their money after they subscribe.

Can an investor require redemption on the timetable the documents actually contain? Or is the capital committed to an investment program, with cash returned through distributions and eventual realizations rather than an investor-controlled exit? That distinction affects the documents, the governance, the liquidity arrangements, and the Cayman regulatory analysis. It matters as much to a family office deciding whether to invest as it does to a sponsor deciding what to launch.

Open-ended does not mean cash on demand

An open-ended fund gives investors redemption rights under its governing terms. Those rights need not provide daily liquidity, or even frequent liquidity. The documents may provide dealing dates, advance notice, an initial lock-up, a limit on the amount that can be redeemed at one time, and circumstances in which dealing can be suspended. A redemption gate limits withdrawals. A suspension can stop the process for a period. The details decide what an investor can actually expect. How to read those details is the subject of Cayman Fund Liquidity Terms.

Three clocks should be kept separate, even in a first conversation. When can the investor submit a request? When is the redemption effective, and the value fixed? When must the fund pay? A favorable answer to the first question is not a favorable answer to the third.

A closed-ended fund generally does not give investors a right to require redemption at their option. The documents govern commitments, capital calls where the strategy uses them, distributions, the term, and the winding down. A permitted transfer to another investor is not the same thing as a right to receive cash from the fund. What happens when a closed-ended vehicle distributes assets rather than cash is a separate practical question, discussed in Winding Down an Investment Fund.

Neither model is inherently better. The issue is whether the rights match the investment program and the investor’s needs.

The regulatory distinction is not company versus partnership

Under the Mutual Funds Act (2025 Revision), a mutual fund is a company, unit trust, or partnership that issues equity interests, the purpose or effect of which is the pooling of investor funds with the aim of spreading investment risks and enabling investors to receive profits or gains from the investments. An equity interest, for that Act, carries an entitlement to participate in the profits or gains and is redeemable or repurchasable at the option of the investor. The definition excludes debt and certain alternative instruments, and a Cayman company must also be read against the Companies Act cross-reference in the definition. The full definition, the scope provisions, and the exclusions still have to be applied.

Under the Private Funds Act (2025 Revision), an investment interest carries an entitlement to participate in the profits or gains and is not redeemable or repurchasable at the option of the investor. A private fund offers or issues those interests so that investors can receive profits or gains from the acquisition, holding, management, or disposal of investments, the holders do not have day-to-day control over the investments, and the investments are managed as a whole by or on behalf of the operator, directly or indirectly, for a reward based on the assets, profits, or gains. The Act does not apply to a regulated mutual fund. Non-fund arrangements and the other exclusions still have to be applied.

That is why “mutual fund” in the Cayman statute should not be read as a U.S. retail mutual fund, and why “private fund” under Cayman legislation is not interchangeable with the way the SEC uses “private fund” in the U.S. securities laws. The labels travel. The statutes do not.

Open-ended and closed-ended models compared
Question Open-ended model Closed-ended model
How does an investor ordinarily exit? Through contractual redemption rights, subject to their conditions. Through distributions, realizations, and winding down. Transfers may be separately permitted.
Which Cayman statute is ordinarily relevant? The Mutual Funds Act, if the vehicle falls within its scope. The Private Funds Act, if the vehicle falls within its scope.
What needs particular drafting attention? Dealing dates, notice, valuation, gates, suspensions, and payment timing. Commitments, the investment period, distributions, extensions, and termination.

Companies are commonly used for open-ended strategies, and exempted limited partnerships are commonly used for closed-ended strategies. Those are structuring conventions, not a rule that a company must be open-ended or a partnership must be closed-ended. Both statutes contemplate more than one legal form: company, partnership, and unit trust.

The asset label does not settle the question either. A receivables strategy can be structured with or without an investor redemption right. Real estate may call for patient capital, but the rights in the documents have to drive the analysis, not the word “property” in a presentation. A stated investment horizon or a renewal date is also not enough to classify a fund. A vehicle can have a multi-year horizon and still allow redemptions along the way. Lock-ups and hybrid arrangements need particular care. The statutory analysis cannot be reduced to the fund’s marketing label.

CIMA registration and commercial liquidity are different questions

A common registration route for an open-ended fund is section 4(3) of the Mutual Funds Act. One branch of that section is a minimum aggregate equity interest purchasable by a prospective investor of CI$80,000, or its equivalent in another currency. CIMA describes that figure as US$100,000. Another branch is a listing on a stock exchange, including an over-the-counter market, specified by the Authority. A master fund has a corresponding minimum or listing condition. Registration, the annual fee, and, unless an exemption is granted, prescribed details of the offering document are part of the same section. It would be incorrect to treat CI$80,000 as a universal minimum for every Cayman fund.

CIMA’s investment-funds FAQ states that an administered fund under section 4(1)(b), whose principal office is provided by a licensed mutual fund administrator, does not have that minimum. A limited investor fund under section 4(4) is a different category again: the equity interests are held by not more than fifteen investors, a majority of whom are capable of appointing or removing the operator, and the fund is registered. A licensed mutual fund under section 4(1)(a) has its own conditions. The statute, rather than a single dollar figure, is the analysis.

The same FAQ states that the Private Funds Act does not impose a minimum investment. That does not make a person eligible under the fund’s own terms, or under the rules that govern an offering in the United States or elsewhere.

For a private fund that is required to register, the launch plan has two different clocks, and Cayman counsel should run them. The application is due within twenty-one days after the fund accepts capital commitments from investors for the purposes of investments. Separately, the fund may not accept capital contributions from investors in respect of investments until the Authority has registered it. The Act allows oral or written communications, and agreements with high-net-worth or sophisticated persons as those terms are used in the statute, before the application is filed. Those permissions are not a permission to take investment money early.

Registration does not create an investor redemption right. A redemption clause does not create the cash required to honor it. Where the money sits — in the same vehicle, in a feeder, or beside a parallel fund — is a different question, covered in Cayman Feeder vs. Parallel Funds.

U.S. investors introduce a separate analysis

A Cayman vehicle does not replace U.S. securities-law analysis when U.S. capital or U.S. management is involved. Offering exemptions, Investment Company Act exclusions, and the adviser’s registration or exemption position are separate workstreams. An investor questionnaire needs to reflect the applicable U.S. requirements, not merely a Cayman subscription minimum. Where non-U.S. investors are approached offshore, Regulation S is one of those workstreams. It is not a label that attaches automatically because the vehicle is Cayman.

Tax classification is another distinct question. A foreign vehicle’s local legal form does not finish the U.S. tax analysis. Where a U.S. investor holds an interest in a foreign corporation classified as a passive foreign investment company, reporting and election issues can become significant. A qualified electing fund election is not a sentence to insert in a subscription agreement. The IRS instructions for Form 8621 call for annual information supporting the investor’s share of ordinary earnings and net capital gain. Whether that information can be obtained from the underlying investments should be addressed with the appropriate tax advisers before subscriptions are accepted. The PFIC mechanics are discussed in PFIC Rules for Cross-Border Investors.

Sponsors should avoid any assurance that a Cayman vehicle eliminates U.S. income tax, withholding, filing obligations, or estate-tax exposure. The answer depends on the investors, the classification, the underlying activities, and the rest of the structure.

What to settle before the first subscription

  1. Investor rights. Redemption rights or committed capital, including the precise conditions of exit. The mechanics are in the liquidity note.
  2. Investment liquidity. Where the cash will come from, and whether the timetable in the documents can be supported.
  3. Structure. Which entity holds each investment, and whether vehicles feed into one another or invest alongside each other.
  4. Responsibilities. Operator, manager, administrator, auditor, and compliance. Those seats are separated in Who Does What in a Cayman Fund.
  5. Disclosure. Compensation across the structure, conflicts, valuation, and the information investors’ tax advisers will need.
  6. Coordination. Cayman registration with qualified Cayman counsel, U.S. requirements with U.S. counsel, and the laws of the places where investments are made or investors are approached.

The objective is not the largest structure chart. It is a structure in which the exit rights, the assets, the documents, and the regulatory treatment tell the same story.
