Cross-Border, International & Wealth Structuring

When a U.S. Trust Form Is Used for a Foreign Settlor

By Rafael Recalde — November 22, 2026

Recalde Law Firm, P.A. is a Florida law firm with an office in Miami, Florida. We advise on United States law only. We do not practice Mexican law, and we do not give Mexican tax or succession advice. We do not practice the law of any other country. Formation of a non-U.S. entity, and the local law of that jurisdiction, are matters for local counsel. We handle the U.S. architecture: classification, the documents a U.S. custodian and a U.S. court will read, and coordination with the family's tax adviser.

A revocable-trust form written for a Florida couple is a coherent document for that couple. It assumes a settlor who is a U.S. person, a lifetime gift exclusion, a reason to create present interests for the annual exclusion, and a set of powers that make the settlor the income-tax owner under sections 671 through 679. Handed to a Mexican parent who is a nonresident and not a U.S. citizen, the same clauses answer questions the parent does not have, and they can miss the one clause section 672(f) actually requires. The form is not wrong. It is written for a different donor.

The U.S. person's basic exclusion is $15 million for 2026, indexed afterward. The clauses below are how a U.S. form spends that exclusion, or avoids spending it. A nonresident settlor does not compute U.S. gift tax with that exclusion. The RIA or family office that receives the draft can catch the mismatch before anyone signs.

Crummey powers

A Crummey power gives a beneficiary a present right to withdraw a contribution, so that a gift in trust can be a present interest and can use the annual exclusion under section 2503(b). That machinery matters when the gift is subject to U.S. gift tax. For a U.S. donor funding an insurance trust, it is discussed in Crummey letters.

Section 2501(a)(2) provides that a nonresident who is not a U.S. citizen is not subject to U.S. gift tax on a transfer of intangible property. Stock, including shares of a foreign company, is the usual intangible in these files. A gift of that stock, outright or in trust, generally produces no U.S. gift tax and no use of a lifetime exclusion. A withdrawal right is not buying an annual exclusion the transfer needed. It does have U.S. consequences of its own. In the beneficiary's hands it can be a general power of appointment, with section 2514 on a lapse above the greater of $5,000 or 5 percent, and with section 2041 if the beneficiary dies holding it. A beneficiary who holds the power can be an owner of that slice under section 678. And a right in a U.S. child to withdraw during the parent's life can be inconsistent with a section 672(f) trust that is supposed to distribute only to the parent or the parent's spouse. For a gift of non-U.S.-situs intangible property, the power is unnecessary for U.S. gift tax and can work against the trust the family meant to sign.

U.S. real estate and tangible property located in the United States are different gifts. A nonresident can owe U.S. gift tax on those transfers, and there is no lifetime unified credit of the kind a U.S. person has. A present-interest exclusion can matter there. The deed should not use one withdrawal article for every asset the parent might give.

Formulas that mention the exclusion

U.S. forms often give "the largest amount that can pass free of federal gift tax by reason of the applicable credit," or they allocate the settlor's generation-skipping exemption in the same sentence. Those words point at a U.S. person's credit. For 2026 that basic exclusion is $15 million, indexed afterward. A nonresident settlor does not have that credit against gifts of U.S.-situs property, and a gift of foreign intangible property is generally outside U.S. gift tax entirely. A formula that cannot find a credit can fail to describe a gift, or it can be read as giving more than the parent was shown on a diagram. The transfer should name the property. The tax adviser says whether any U.S. gift-tax return is due. The generation-skipping clause is rewritten the same way, from the people who can receive, rather than from a U.S. exemption allocation the settlor may not be in a position to make.

Settlor control, and the clause that was meant to create a grantor trust

A U.S. form often names the settlor as trustee, or gives the settlor a power to substitute assets of equal value under section 675(4)(C), so the settlor is the income-tax owner. Section 672(f) blocks that result for a foreign settlor. The foreign settlor is the owner only through the exceptions: a power to revest exercisable alone, or distributions during life only to the settlor or the settlor's spouse. A swap power does not stand in for either exception. A trust that relies on the swap power, and on nothing else, is generally a foreign non-grantor trust from the start, with the distribution rules that apply to U.S. beneficiaries of an FNGT. See FGT versus FNGT.

Control also decides whether the trust is domestic. If the settlor is not a U.S. person and holds the substantial decisions, the control test in section 7701 is often failed, and the trust is foreign. That can be exactly what the family wants during life. The same clause still in the deed after death is the one the drafter rewrites, so that U.S. persons hold the substantial decisions from that day. The design that moves those decisions to U.S. persons at death is in a foreign grantor trust that becomes a Wyoming dynasty trust. Retained powers are read a second time for estate tax. A power to revoke can include U.S.-situs assets in a nonresident's gross estate under sections 2036 and 2038. The limited exemption is the section 2102(b) credit. Foreign-situs property is generally outside that estate. The form's trustee clause should be read against both statutes, not against the caption "revocable living trust."

What the reviewer changes

Strike the Crummey article unless a particular gift is a taxable gift of U.S.-situs property and a present interest is wanted for that gift. Replace exclusion formulas with a description of the property transferred. Build grantor status, if it is wanted, on a section 672(f) exception the parent can actually live with, and say who holds each substantial decision before death and after. Then ask Mexican counsel to read the same deed. A U.S. form that has not been through that pass is a Florida estate plan with a Mexican signature.

Write as the adviser, or as the family.
