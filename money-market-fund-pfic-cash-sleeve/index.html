Fund Formation & Cross-Border Structuring

The Portfolio Holding You Cannot Elect Over

By Rafael Recalde — November 12, 2026

In this series. This is the lower tier an election at the top does not reach, and the one the manager cannot sign for. The first year the position is held is the year the election is due. The practice page is Cayman funds.

A fund that parks cash in a third-party money-market or short-term bond fund has, for U.S. purposes, often acquired stock of a foreign corporation with passive income, passive assets, and limited liability for its holders. The sponsor of that corporation has no reason to produce a bespoke U.S. statement for one holder among thousands. The qualified electing fund election the documents cannot compel is the election that does not get made.

When the sleeve is a PFIC

Section 1297(a) applies to a foreign corporation. If seventy-five percent or more of its gross income is passive, or if the average percentage of its assets that produce passive income or are held to produce it is at least fifty percent, it is a PFIC. A vehicle whose assets and income are short-term debt, deposits, and similar passive items will often meet both tests. “Often” is the observation. The tests are annual and factual. A marketing name does not classify the vehicle.

A domestic fund that is not a foreign corporation is not a PFIC. The issue is a foreign corporate sleeve. It is not the words “money market.”

The upper fund, if it is itself a foreign corporation holding securities, may be a PFIC whether the cash sits in a second fund or on its own books. This note is about the additional PFIC. Holding the instruments directly does not, by itself, take the upper fund out of section 1297. Cash and debt instruments are the sort of assets that count toward the passive tests. Moving them onto the upper fund's balance sheet can change the upper fund's own computation. It removes a separate foreign corporation the manager does not control.

What does not take the holding out

Section 1297 has no de minimis for a small sleeve. The filing thresholds in Treasury Regulation section 1.1298-1(c)(2), restated in the Instructions for Form 8621 (December 2025), can excuse Form 8621 for a section 1291 fund if, on the last day of the year, aggregate PFIC value is $25,000 or less, or $50,000 or less on a joint return, or the stock is indirect stock through a PFIC worth $5,000 or less. The exception also requires that the shareholder have no excess distribution and no disposition gain treated as an excess distribution, and that no qualified electing fund election have been made for that fund. It does not stop the vehicle from being a PFIC. A sleeve that is bought and later sold is a poor candidate for the exception, because the disposition turns it off.

There is a short-term exception, and it is a filing exception. Treasury Regulation section 1.1298-1(c)(7) can excuse Form 8621 for a section 1291 fund when the shareholder acquired it in the taxable year or the immediately preceding year, was a shareholder for a total of thirty days or less in a measuring period that runs from twenty-nine days before the shareholder's year to twenty-nine days after it, and is not treated as receiving an excess distribution, including gain on a disposition. A cash sleeve held for a month, or sold at a gain, does not fit. The exception does not stop the vehicle from being a PFIC, and it does not produce a qualified electing fund election.

Section 1298(b)(2) is a start-up year rule for the corporation's first taxable year with gross income. Section 1298(b)(3) is a change-of-business rule. Each requires, among other conditions, that it be established to the satisfaction of the Secretary that the corporation will not be a PFIC for either of the next two taxable years, and that the corporation in fact not be a PFIC for those years. Those conditions look at the corporation's own following years. A vehicle that continues to hold passive assets does not meet them because a shareholder's holding happened to be short. Section 1298(b)(1) treats stock as PFIC stock if the corporation was a PFIC at any time in the shareholder's holding period and was not a qualified electing fund, unless the shareholder makes the gain-recognition election that paragraph describes.

Section 1296 provides a mark-to-market election for marketable stock. It is a different election, made by the shareholder, and it does not remove PFIC status. The December 2025 Form 8621 instructions state that a mark-to-market election made in a year other than the first year the shareholder holds the stock, with no qualified electing fund election in effect, treats the stock as sold at fair market value and treats the gain as an excess distribution. We describe that coordination so the election is not mistaken for an exemption or for a short-holding rule. We do not recommend the election, and we do not recommend a product or a provider.

The fix is a policy, and it is made before the subscription

The annual information statement is signed by the PFIC or an authorized representative, or it is passed on by an intermediary who received the PFIC's statement. A sponsor with a large holder base does not take on a U.S. computation and an inspection undertaking for one investing fund. A minority holder cannot sign the statement in the PFIC's name. Cooperation is not authority. That distinction is the information-rights note.

Holding the instruments in the fund that the manager does control, as a matter of investment policy, avoids acquiring the stock of a foreign corporation the manager cannot bind. The policy has to be in place before the subscription. A cleanup after a year of lower-tier positions arrives in a year for which the first-year election rule has already started, which is the first-year cliff. Reclassifying someone else's corporate fund as a partnership is not available to a holder who does not control it.

This is a description of the U.S. classification problem. It is not advice to buy or sell an instrument, and it is not a view on credit, liquidity, or custody. Those decisions belong to the manager, and the operational mechanics of holding assets belong to the persons engaged to perform them.
