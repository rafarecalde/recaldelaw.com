The Stretch IRA and the 10-Year Rule

By Rafael Recalde — November 22, 2026

Recalde Law Firm, P.A. is a Florida law firm with an office in Miami, Florida. We advise on United States law. What follows is general. It describes no family's facts and no family's numbers. Tax results are confirmed with the family's tax adviser. We advise on the legal structure.

For many years, a person who inherited an IRA could take distributions over his or her own life expectancy. Families called that a stretch. The SECURE Act changed the general rule. Under section 401(a)(9), a designated beneficiary who is not an eligible designated beneficiary empties the account by the end of the year that contains the tenth anniversary of the owner's death. Age does not lengthen that period.

The ten years, and the years inside them

The outside date is the tenth year. Whether the beneficiary must also take a distribution in each of the intervening years depends on whether the owner died before or after the required beginning date. The regulations apply an annual distribution during those years when the owner had already reached that date, and they still require the account to be empty by the end of year ten. When death is before that date, the planning point is the tenth year. The tax adviser applies the regulations to the owner's facts. This note does not restate the transition relief that covered certain missed distributions in earlier years.

The required beginning date is set from the applicable age in section 401(a)(9). For owners reaching it in this period, that age is 73. It is 75 for a younger group, based on birth year. The tax adviser uses the owner's birth year rather than a single age remembered from a prior statute.

Who can still use a life expectancy

An eligible designated beneficiary is a defined category. It includes the surviving spouse. It includes the owner's child who has not reached majority, and only while that child is a minor. The regulations state the age they treat as majority. The tax adviser applies that age. It is not the child's life. It includes a beneficiary who is disabled, and a beneficiary who is chronically ill, under the statute's definitions. It includes a beneficiary who is not more than ten years younger than the owner.

A surviving spouse may have a further choice the others do not: rolling the account into the spouse's own IRA, when the plan or the IRA allows it. That rollover starts a new owner clock. Remaining a beneficiary is the other choice. The tax adviser compares them. A spouse who rolls the account over is no longer on the inherited-account calendar.

The youngest-beneficiary myth

Naming the youngest child, or a grandchild, does not restore a lifetime stretch. A designated beneficiary who is outside the eligible categories is on the ten-year rule at 25 and at 60. A grandchild is not an eligible designated beneficiary merely by being young. The minor-child category is the owner's child, and only during minority. A grandchild almost never falls within "not more than ten years younger."

Under the old stretch, when a trust was the beneficiary and the trust was looked through, the measuring life was generally the oldest beneficiary who counted, not the youngest. Youth was never a reason to list the youngest name and ignore the others. Naming a trust, and the difference between a conduit trust and an accumulation trust, is covered in naming a trust as IRA or 401(k) beneficiary.

The estate, and a charity, are not designated beneficiaries. If the owner died before the required beginning date, the five-year rule applies. If the owner died on or after that date, the payout runs over the owner's remaining single life expectancy, which can be longer than ten years. An account payable to the estate also passes through probate. A beneficiary form that still says "Estate" is worth reading against this rule before it is left in place. Deferred compensation that is not a qualified plan is a different contract entirely, covered in nonqualified deferred compensation.
