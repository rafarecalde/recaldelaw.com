Nonqualified Deferred Compensation Is Not an IRA

By Rafael Recalde — November 22, 2026

Recalde Law Firm, P.A. is a Florida law firm with an office in Miami, Florida. We advise on United States law. What follows is general. It describes no family's facts and no family's numbers. Tax results are confirmed with the family's tax adviser. We advise on the legal structure.

Nonqualified deferred compensation is the employer's contractual promise to pay compensation later. It is not an IRA. It is not a 401(k) or another qualified plan. The account, if the plan keeps a bookkeeping account, is not a custodial account the beneficiary can roll over. There is no ten-year rule under section 401(a)(9), and there is no stretch. Those rules are for retirement accounts, covered in the stretch IRA and the 10-year rule. This benefit pays on the plan's terms.

How the payout is taxed

The recipient includes the payment as ordinary income. When the employee has died, the unpaid amount is income in respect of a decedent under section 691. Section 1014(c) provides that the basis step-up does not apply to income in respect of a decedent. The family does not inherit the deferred pay free of income tax. Section 691(c) allows a deduction related to estate tax attributable to the same item. The tax adviser computes that deduction. It is not a second step-up.

Section 409A is the timing statute for the employee's own deferrals and for the plan's payment events. Death is a payment event the plan may use. The schedule in the plan controls the installments or the lump sum. This note does not restate section 409A. Employment-tax timing on the deferral is its own computation, and it does not follow the withholding pattern of an IRA distribution.

The promise is unsecured. The beneficiary is a creditor of the employer for the unpaid amount. If the employer does not pay, there is no custodian holding segregated assets the way an IRA custodian does.

Why the beneficiary line matters

A designation of "Estate" makes the benefit payable to the probate estate. It is then a probate asset. Estate creditors may reach it to the extent probate law allows. The income is reported by the estate, or by the beneficiary who receives it from the estate, under the fiduciary income-tax rules. A marital deduction for estate tax requires that the interest pass to the surviving spouse in a form that qualifies. Paying the estate does not itself create that deduction. The will or the revocable trust can still carry the residue to the spouse, and the deduction may then be available, with the delay and the creditor exposure of probate included. The income tax follows the payment either way.

A designation of the spouse pays the spouse. The spouse includes the ordinary income. For estate tax, an outright payment to the surviving spouse is the simplest interest that qualifies for the marital deduction, when the amount is included in the estate. For 2026 the basic exclusion is $15 million per person, indexed afterward. The deduction matters when the estate is over the exclusion that actually applies. It does not erase the income tax.

A designation of a trust pays the trustee, if the form identifies the trust in the way the plan administrator will accept. Whether the trust qualifies for a marital deduction, or is a bypass trust that uses the exclusion on purpose, is read from the instrument. A trust named by habit, because the revocable trust is the center of the estate plan, can send deferred compensation into a trust that was written for probate assets. The beneficiary form is reviewed with the plan and with the trust. Amending the trust later does not amend the form, which is the same coordination point as in naming a trust as IRA beneficiary.
