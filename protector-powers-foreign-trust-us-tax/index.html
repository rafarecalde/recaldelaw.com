Cross-Border, International & Wealth Structuring

Protectors and Reserved Powers in a Foreign Trust

By Rafael Recalde — October 30, 2026

A protector is how many foreign trusts are actually governed. The trustee holds legal title. The protector can remove the trustee, consent to a distribution, or approve a change of law. Families add the role so that no single offshore company is the only person who can act, and so that someone who knows the family can intervene if the trustee stops answering. The same clauses are substantial decisions under section 7701, and they can be the strings that keep grantor status or estate inclusion. The role is useful. It is not neutral.

This is a note on architecture, not a form of protector language. The control test is set out in The Court Test and the Control Test. Grantor status for a non-U.S. settlor is in FGT vs. FNGT. The practice page is Foreign Trusts.

What a protector is good for

An independent protector, with a fiduciary duty and no beneficial interest, can replace a trustee who will not instruct a bank, approve a change of governing law if the deed allows it, and stand between branches of a family that do not want the settlor to be the only person with a veto. That is governance. It is the reason the office of protector exists in Cayman, BVI, and similar deeds, and the reason a Florida or Delaware trust sometimes borrows the title. The question for U.S. purposes is which decisions the protector can actually block.

The control test

If a non-U.S. protector must consent to distributions, to the choice of beneficiary, to termination, or to the removal of the trustee, that person holds a substantial decision. U.S. persons then do not control all substantial decisions, and the trust fails the control test. The trust is foreign even if the trustee is in Miami and the deed chooses Florida law. Families discover this when they thought "our U.S. trustee" made the trust domestic. The protector's consent right was the test.

A U.S. protector with those same rights can push the other way. Together with a U.S. court that can supervise administration, the trust may be domestic whether or not the family wanted a foreign trust. Domestic status turns off some foreign-trust reporting and turns on others, including the section 684 question if the trust later becomes foreign again. The protector's passport is not a detail for the KYC file only.

Grantor status

Section 672(f) generally refuses grantor-trust treatment to a foreign person except in narrow cases. One of them is a power, exercisable solely by the grantor and without anyone else's approval, to revest the assets in the grantor. A protector who must approve the grantor's revocation can take the trust out of that exception. The family keeps a protector for governance and loses FGT status without meaning to. The inverse is also common: the settlor is the protector, and the powers are exactly the retained powers that section 672(f) requires — and exactly the powers that sections 2036 and 2038 use to include U.S.-situs assets in the estate at death. See When the Foreign Grantor Dies and U.S.-Situs Assets Inside a Foreign Trust.

A U.S. person who holds protector powers and who transferred property to the trust can be an owner under sections 671 through 679, including section 679 if the trust is foreign and has a U.S. beneficiary. That person then has a Form 3520-A problem, not merely a governance role. See Forms 3520 and 3520-A.

Estate inclusion and powers of appointment

A settlor-protector who can add beneficiaries, veto distributions, or take the property back has retained a power over U.S.-situs assets. For a non-resident alien, those assets are the U.S. estate-tax problem. A protector who is a U.S. person and who can appoint property to himself, his estate, or his creditors can have a general power of appointment under section 2041, which is an estate-inclusion rule for that U.S. person, not for the settlor. The draft that lets the protector "act for the benefit of the family, including himself" is the draft that needs to be reread before it is signed.

The powers that usually survive a U.S. reading are narrower. The protector is not a beneficiary and cannot become one. The protector cannot add himself. Removal and replacement of the trustee is limited to an identified class of institutional trustees, and is fiduciary. Consent rights that are substantial decisions are given only to a person whose residence matches the foreign-or-domestic result the family actually wants. A letter of wishes can say what the settlor hopes. If the trustee treats the letter as an instruction it must follow, the letter is a retained power wearing different paper. Substance is the test the classification rules already use. The letter does not sit outside them.

How to read an existing protector clause

List every decision the protector can veto or direct. Mark which of those are substantial. Mark the residence and citizenship of the person who holds them today, and of the successor. Then ask two questions only: does this power keep the trust foreign or make it domestic, and does this power belong to the settlor in a way that continues grantor status or estate inclusion. If the answers are accidents, the clause is the amendment, not the rest of the deed. We coordinate that reading with the trustee and with tax counsel. We do not leave the protector as a name on a schedule.
