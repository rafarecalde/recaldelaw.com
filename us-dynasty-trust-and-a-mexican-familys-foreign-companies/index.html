Cross-Border, International & Wealth Structuring

Why a U.S. Dynasty Trust Changes the Tax on a Mexican Family's Foreign Companies

By Rafael Recalde — November 22, 2026

Recalde Law Firm, P.A. is a Florida law firm with an office in Miami, Florida. We advise on United States law only. We do not practice Mexican law, and we do not give Mexican tax or succession advice. We do not practice the law of any other country. Formation of a non-U.S. entity, and the local law of that jurisdiction, are matters for local counsel. We handle the U.S. architecture: classification, the documents a U.S. custodian and a U.S. court will read, and coordination with the family's tax adviser.

An adviser will sometimes open a file for a Mexican family by forming a U.S. dynasty trust and retitling the family's BVI companies, or a Canadian limited partnership, into it. The phrase "dynasty" describes a long-term trust for children and grandchildren. It does not describe the U.S. income-tax owner. A domestic trust is a U.S. person. The day that trust owns the foreign company, the U.S. anti-deferral rules have a shareholder or a partner they did not have when the Mexican parent owned the same company.

This note is that income-tax change. It is written for the RIA, the private banker, and the family office that already knows the family. Mexican tax is for Mexican counsel. The U.S. person's basic exclusion, $15 million for 2026 and indexed afterward, is a figure for a U.S. citizen or a person domiciled in the United States. It is not the figure that applies to a Mexican parent who is a nonresident and not a U.S. citizen.

What the parent owns today

A Mexican parent who is not a U.S. person can own a BVI company, or an interest in a Canadian limited partnership, without being a U.S. shareholder and without being a U.S. partner. Subpart F, net CFC tested income (formerly GILTI), and the PFIC rules are aimed at U.S. persons. They do not attach to the parent merely because the company is foreign. U.S. estate tax on that parent is a situs question. Stock of a foreign corporation is generally not U.S.-situs property. The limited exemption for a nonresident's U.S.-situs assets, the credit under section 2102(b), is a separate exposure, discussed in U.S.-situs assets inside a foreign trust. It is not a reason to move foreign companies into a U.S. trust.

What changes when a domestic trust is the owner

Section 7701(a)(30) treats a domestic trust as a U.S. person. A trust is domestic when a U.S. court can exercise primary supervision and one or more U.S. persons control all substantial decisions. A Wyoming or Florida dynasty trust that meets both tests is that U.S. person, even if every beneficiary lives in Mexico City.

A BVI company is a foreign corporation. If U.S. shareholders own more than half of it, it is a controlled foreign corporation under section 957. A U.S. shareholder, for that purpose, includes a U.S. person who owns at least 10 percent of the vote or the value. A trust that owns the company outright is that shareholder. This paragraph assumes the domestic trust is a non-grantor trust, or a grantor trust as to a U.S. person. A foreign settlor who holds a section 672(f) power over a domestic trust changes who reports the income. Subpart F income under section 951, and net CFC tested income (formerly GILTI) under section 951A, can then be included every year, whether or not the company pays a dividend. Inclusions follow section 958(a) direct and indirect ownership. Separately, section 958(b), through section 318 constructive ownership, including family attribution from parent to child, can make the company a CFC, or can make a child a U.S. shareholder, for status testing once any U.S. person holds shares directly. The 2025 legislation changed downward attribution from foreign persons: section 958(b)(4) was restored, and section 951B was added. The tax adviser should confirm how those rules apply to the year. The longer notes are controlled foreign corporations and net CFC tested income (formerly GILTI).

A foreign corporation with mostly passive income or passive assets can also be a PFIC under section 1297. Where the trust is a 10 percent U.S. shareholder of a CFC, the CFC/PFIC overlap rule in section 1297(d) generally keeps that stock on the CFC side for the period the overlap applies. Where the trust's ownership stays below that line, or the overlap does not cover the year, the PFIC rules in section 1291, or a QEF or mark-to-market election, are the ones the tax adviser reads. The point for the adviser in the room is simpler than the election menu. Passive foreign stock that sat quietly under a Mexican parent can become an annual U.S. inclusion, or a PFIC computation, once a U.S. trust owns it.

A Canadian limited partnership is a classification question first

A Canadian limited partnership is not automatically a corporation and not automatically a partnership for U.S. tax. Classification follows the check-the-box rules, which are covered in the Form 8832 election and in Canada LPs in cross-border structures. If the partnership classification holds, section 702 includes each partner's share of income currently. A domestic trust that is a partner picks up that share. Form 5471 and Form 8621 are the reports for a foreign corporation. Form 8865 is the report for an interest in a foreign partnership, including the Canadian limited partnership. If the trust is the only owner and the entity is disregarded, the trust is treated as owning what sits underneath, and those assets get their own classification. A BVI general partner owned by the same trust is a second foreign corporation, with the CFC and PFIC questions above.

What to ask before the trust is funded

Ask what the parent owns, and who would own it the day after funding. A U.S. trust is the right owner for an asset the family wants a U.S. person to own, with the inclusions that follow. It is a poor first step for a stack of foreign companies whose U.S. income-tax life is quiet only because the owner is not a U.S. person. The alternative that keeps the parent as the owner during life, and moves the trust to U.S. status at death, is described in a foreign grantor trust that becomes a Wyoming dynasty trust. The BVI company as a U.S. estate-tax holding tool, which is a different use, is in BVI companies and Mexican families.
