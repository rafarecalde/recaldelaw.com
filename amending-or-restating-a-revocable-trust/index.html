Amending or Restating a Revocable Trust

By Rafael Recalde — November 22, 2026

Recalde Law Firm, P.A. is a Florida law firm with an office in Miami, Florida. We advise on United States law. What follows is general. It describes no family's facts and no family's numbers. Tax results are confirmed with the family's tax adviser. We advise on the legal structure.

A revocable trust is meant to be changed as the family changes. The choice is among a short amendment, an amendment and restatement, and a new trust. The choice is about identity. The same trust can be rewritten. A new trust is a different owner, and every asset has to be moved.

Who may change it

Section 736.0602 provides that the settlor may revoke or amend a revocable trust unless the terms say the trust is irrevocable. If the instrument states a method, the settlor uses that method, in substantial compliance with it. If the instrument states no method, the statute allows a later will or codicil that expressly refers to the trust, or specifically devises property that would have passed under the trust, or any other method that shows the settlor's intent by clear and convincing evidence. An instrument signed before the Florida Trust Code took effect is read on its own terms. The subsection that grants the default power does not apply to those earlier instruments.

The settlor has to have capacity. A restatement signed without capacity does not amend anything. An agent under a power of attorney may amend a trust only as section 709.2202 allows, which means the power of attorney has to grant that authority. A guardian of the property acts only within section 744.441. After the settlor's death the trust is irrevocable. The successor trustee administers the terms that exist. He or she does not write new ones.

If two settlors funded the trust, section 736.0602(2) divides the power. As to community property, either spouse may revoke, and both must amend. As to other property, each settlor may revoke or amend the portion attributable to that settlor's contribution. A Florida community property trust makes that distinction concrete. It is covered in Florida community property trusts.

Amendment, restatement, new trust

An amendment changes named sections and leaves the rest of the instrument in place. It fits a new successor trustee, a new grandchild, or a corrected legal description. Several amendments can be read together. A stack that no one can reconcile is the moment a restatement is the clearer document.

An amendment and restatement replaces the whole text and states that it amends and restates the trust created on the original date. The trust keeps its name and its date of creation. Assets already titled in the trustee of that trust stay where they are. The pour-over will that names that trust still pours into it. The restatement should state that the trust continues, so a reader does not treat the document as a revocation that returns title to the settlor.

During the settlor's life, a revocable trust usually reports under the settlor's Social Security number as a grantor trust. A new trust is a different trust, and a different grantee and owner of record on the titles and the accounts. Accounts are retitled. Deeds are rerecorded. Beneficiary forms are redone. That path fits when the settlors are different people, or when the old trust was never funded and there is nothing to retitle. It is an expensive path when the only problem is outdated distributive language and the accounts are already correct. A document that says the old trust is revoked and a new one is created is a new trust, whatever the cover calls it.

What a restatement does not update

Life insurance, IRAs, and deferred compensation pay according to their beneficiary forms. Restating the trust does not change those forms. The retirement-account form is discussed in naming a trust as IRA beneficiary, and the deferred-compensation form in nonqualified deferred compensation. A deed of Florida homestead, if a new trust is the grantee, needs the spouse's joinder when the property is homestead. That requirement is in Florida homestead and transfers into a trust. A restatement of a trust that already holds the homestead does not, by itself, require a new deed.
