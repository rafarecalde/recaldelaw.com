Series LLC or a New SPV for Every Deal?

By Rafael Recalde — October 17, 2026

A sponsor who expects to do more than a handful of deals faces a structural choice early. Each investment can go into its own newly formed LLC. Or the sponsor can form one Delaware series LLC and put each deal into its own series. Both approaches work, and both are common. The right answer depends mostly on how many deals the sponsor expects to do, how quickly, and who sits on the other side of those deals.

How a Delaware Series LLC Works

Section 18-215 of the Delaware Limited Liability Company Act lets an LLC agreement establish one or more designated series of members, managers, LLC interests, or assets. Each series can have its own rights, powers, and duties with respect to specified property or obligations, and its own business purpose or investment objective. In a sponsor platform, that means one master entity with a master operating agreement, and a separate series for each deal, with its own investors, its own investment, and its own economics.

The point of the structure is ring-fencing. Under § 18-215(b), the debts and liabilities of a protected series can be enforced only against that series' assets, and not against the master LLC or any other series, if three conditions are met:

  • the records maintained for the series account for its assets separately from the other assets of the LLC and of any other series;
  • the LLC agreement provides for the limitation; and
  • the certificate of formation gives notice of the limitation on liabilities of a series.

Unless the LLC agreement says otherwise, a protected series can contract, hold title to assets, and sue and be sued in its own name. A member or manager of a series is not personally liable for the series' obligations solely because of that role.

Delaware also offers a registered series under § 18-218. A registered series is formed by filing a certificate of registered series with the Secretary of State, so it appears on the public record, which some banks and counterparties prefer. Under 6 Del. C. § 18-1107, each registered series pays an annual tax of $100, compared with $400 for each separate LLC, starting with the 2026 tax year.

Why Sponsors Use It

The appeal is cost and speed. The master operating agreement is negotiated once. Each new deal is documented with a series designation or supplement that sets out the deal-specific terms: the investment, the investors in that series, the carry and any fee, and anything unusual about the deal. There is no new certificate of formation for a protected series, and the annual Delaware cost per deal is lower. Investors see the same terms deal after deal, which makes them easier to review. For a platform that expects to launch SPVs regularly, often with a repeat investor base, that adds up.

Where Separate Entities Are Simpler

The series structure asks more of the people outside it. Some banks are slower to open an account in the name of a series, or won't do it at all. A portfolio company's counsel may ask questions about a cap table entry for "XYZ Ventures LLC – Series 7," and some sellers or lead investors will simply prefer a standalone entity. Not every state has a series statute, and how a series is treated outside Delaware, including in a bankruptcy, is less settled than for an ordinary LLC. A sponsor that wants no friction with a particular counterparty can always form a separate LLC for that deal.

There are also questions the documents have to answer, rather than assume:

  • Tax. Confirm with the tax advisers how each series will be classified, whether it files its own return and issues its own K-1s, and whether it needs its own EIN.
  • Securities filings. Decide how each series' offering will be treated for Regulation D and Form D purposes, and document the position.
  • Investment Company Act. Decide whether each series' investors will be counted separately toward the 100-investor or 250-investor limit, and support that position in the documents. The limits are covered in Investment Company Act Limits for SPVs.

The Shield Depends on the Records

The statute protects a series to the extent its records account for its assets separately. That is a bookkeeping obligation, not a formality. A sponsor that runs every deal's cash through one master account, pays one series' expenses from another series' funds, or keeps one ledger for the whole platform weakens the protection the structure is supposed to provide. Separate bank accounts for each series, where the bank allows it, separate ledgers, and expense allocations written into the operating agreement are what make the ring-fence hold.

The same discipline applies to signatures. Contracts for a deal should be signed in the name of that series, not the master LLC, and the series designation should be in place before the series signs anything or takes in investor money.

How to Choose

The decision usually comes down to volume and pace.

  • A few large deals a year, with institutional co-investors or demanding counterparties: separate LLCs are usually simpler. The added cost per deal is small relative to the deal, and nobody has to learn how a series works.
  • Many smaller deals, launched quickly, with a repeat investor base or an online platform: a series LLC tends to pay for itself in time and cost.
  • A mix: some sponsors use a series LLC as the default and form a standalone LLC when a bank, a portfolio company, or a lead investor needs one. The master operating agreement and the standalone SPV agreement can share most of their terms.
