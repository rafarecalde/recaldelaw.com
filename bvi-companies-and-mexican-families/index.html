Cross-Border, International & Wealth Structuring

BVI Companies and Mexican Families

By Rafael Recalde — November 22, 2026

Recalde Law Firm, P.A. is a Florida law firm with an office in Miami, Florida. We advise on United States law only. We do not practice Mexican law, and we do not give Mexican tax or succession advice. We do not practice the law of any other country. Formation of a non-U.S. entity, and the local law of that jurisdiction, are matters for local counsel. We handle the U.S. architecture: classification, the documents a U.S. custodian and a U.S. court will read, and coordination with the family's tax adviser.

A BVI company shows up in Mexican family files for a U.S. reason and a holding reason. The U.S. reason is estate tax. A nonresident who is not a U.S. citizen is taxed on U.S.-situs property, with the limited exemption from the credit in section 2102(b). Stock of a domestic corporation is U.S.-situs. Stock of a foreign corporation generally is not. If the parent owns the BVI company, and the company owns the U.S. assets, the asset in the parent's estate is the foreign stock. That is the blocker. It is a situs result. It is not a statement about income tax, in the United States or in Mexico.

Mexican income tax on the same company is a different statute, read by Mexican counsel. We do not practice Mexican law. The notes below say what the U.S. file can and cannot claim.

What the company changes for U.S. estate tax

Section 2104 treats stock of a domestic corporation as property situated in the United States. It does not treat stock of a foreign corporation that way. A BVI company that holds U.S. portfolio stock, or that holds U.S. real estate, is the shareholder of the U.S. stock or the owner of the real estate. The Mexican parent is the shareholder of the BVI company. In the ordinary case the U.S. estate tax looks at the parent's shares, and those shares are foreign. The underlying U.S. assets are not re-characterized as the parent's U.S.-situs property merely because the family can draw a chart that shows them underneath.

Classification can undo the chart. A BVI company limited by shares defaults to a corporation, because all of its members have limited liability, under Treasury Regulation 301.7701-3(b)(2)(i)(B). A single owner makes the company disregarded only by election, not by default. That election is covered in the Form 8832 election. A single-owner company that stays a corporation is still a blocker. The blocker holds only when the company is run as a real company: the formalities are kept, and the parent does not treat the company's assets as the parent's own. The adviser asks for the classification, and for that record, before describing the company as a blocker.

Income tax on a later sale is not the estate-tax question. Section 897(c)(1)(A)(ii) treats an interest in a domestic corporation as a U.S. real property interest. A nonresident's sale of shares in a BVI company that owns U.S. real estate is generally not a section 897 disposition, unless the company has made a section 897(i) election. FIRPTA applies when the company itself sells the real estate. Sections 897(d) and 1445(e) apply when the company distributes that real estate. Estate-tax situs is a separate computation.

What the company does not change in Mexico

Mexico taxes its residents on a worldwide basis, and it has preferential tax regime rules, usually called REFIPRE, for interests in entities that are subject to a low level of tax. A BVI company is the kind of entity those rules were written to reach. A Mexican resident who owns the company, directly or indirectly, or who controls it, can be required to take the company's income into account currently in Mexico, even though the company pays no dividend and even though the U.S. estate-tax reason for using it is sound. Whether the regime applies, how far ownership or control reaches, who the taxed person is, and whether an active-business or other exception is available, are questions in the Mexican statute. Mexican counsel answers them. A U.S. memo that says the company "defers tax" has not answered them.

The U.S. estate-tax blocker and the Mexican current-inclusion rule can both be true. The company can keep U.S.-situs assets out of the parent's U.S. gross estate and still produce income that Mexico taxes to the parent every year. Families who want deferral in Mexico need a Mexican analysis. The BVI company is not that analysis.

When a U.S. person inherits the shares

The blocker is about a nonresident owner. A U.S. child, or a domestic trust, that inherits or is given the shares is a U.S. person owning a foreign corporation. Controlled-foreign-corporation inclusions and the PFIC rules are then in the file, as described in why a U.S. dynasty trust changes the tax on those companies. Inclusions follow section 958(a) direct and indirect ownership. Separately, section 958(b), through section 318 constructive ownership, including family attribution from parent to child, can make the company a CFC, or can make a child a U.S. shareholder, for status testing once any U.S. person holds shares directly. The 2025 legislation changed downward attribution from foreign persons: section 958(b)(4) was restored, and section 951B was added. The tax adviser should confirm how those rules apply to the year. Forms 5471 and 8621 are the reporting names the tax adviser matches to the ownership. The share certificate that solved a U.S. estate-tax question for the parent can be an annual U.S. inclusion for the heir.

If the shares stay with a foreign grantor trust during the parent's life, the parent can remain the relevant owner for U.S. income tax under section 672(f), and the U.S. children are not yet the shareholders. That design, and the change at death, is in a foreign grantor trust that becomes a Wyoming dynasty trust. The company still needs a Mexican REFIPRE reading for as long as a Mexican resident is the person Mexico treats as owning it.
