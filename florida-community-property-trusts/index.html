Florida Community Property Trusts and Basis at the First Death

By Rafael Recalde — November 22, 2026

Recalde Law Firm, P.A. is a Florida law firm with an office in Miami, Florida. We advise on United States law. What follows is general. It describes no family's facts and no family's numbers. Tax results are confirmed with the family's tax adviser. We advise on the legal structure.

Florida is a common-law property state. Title and the source of funds usually decide which spouse owns an asset. At the first death, the decedent's interest can receive a new basis under section 1014. The survivor's separate interest keeps its old basis. A community property trust is the Florida statute that lets spouses opt chosen assets into community property instead, so that the basis rule for community property can apply to both halves.

The Community Property Trust Act is the part of Chapter 736 that begins at section 736.1501. It applies to a trust created, amended, restated, or modified on or after July 1, 2021.

What the trust has to say

Section 736.1503 sets four requirements. The trust expressly declares that it is a community property trust within the meaning of that part. At least one trustee is a qualified trustee. Both spouses sign it, with the formalities required for a Florida trust. At the beginning, in capital letters, it contains substantially the notice the statute sets out. That notice addresses creditors and other third parties, and the spouses' rights during the marriage, at divorce, and at death. The statute writes the notice. The trust uses it.

A qualified trustee, under section 736.1502, is a natural person who resides in Florida, or a company authorized to act as a trustee in Florida. Both spouses, or either spouse, may also be a trustee. A couple in Miami can serve, so long as one qualified trustee is in place. If one spouse resides in Florida, that spouse can be the qualified trustee.

Section 736.1502 defines the property the qualified trustee holds under the trust during the marriage, and the appreciation and income, as community property for purposes of Florida law.

The basis adjustment the statute is built for

Section 736.1511 provides that, for purposes of applying section 1014(b)(6) of the Internal Revenue Code, a community property trust is considered a trust established under the community property laws of Florida. Section 1014(b)(6) treats the surviving spouse's one-half share of community property as property acquired from the decedent, if at least one-half of the community interest was includible in the decedent's gross estate. The decedent's own half is in the estate and can receive a section 1014 adjustment on that ground. The survivor's half receives an adjustment because of section 1014(b)(6), when that condition is met. Together, that is a basis adjustment to both halves at the first death.

The survivor's half is not included in the estate by reason of the basis rule. Inclusion of the decedent's half is the estate-tax cost, if the estate is large enough to owe tax. For 2026 the basic exclusion is $15 million per person, indexed afterward. A couple under the exclusion that applies to them may owe no estate tax on the included half and still want the basis adjustment. A couple over it has the tax adviser compare the estate tax on the included half with the income tax saved when the property is later sold. The general tradeoff between estate inclusion and basis is in step-up in basis and the swap power.

Florida law supplies the property classification and points at section 1014(b)(6). The federal computation is the tax adviser's. This note states those statutes. It does not point to a published IRS ruling that confirms the result for an elective Florida trust. Families who use the statute do so with the adviser's sign-off in the file.

What else changes

The statutory notice exists because community property changes rights with creditors and between the spouses, during the marriage and at divorce. Those rights are read from the statute at the time, with family-law counsel if a dissolution is underway. They are a reason to read the notice before funding the trust.

Section 736.1511 also provides that community property classified that way by another jurisdiction keeps its character in the trust. A couple who already owns community property from a community-property state, and who now lives in Florida, can move that property into the trust on that footing. Assets that were Florida separate property become community property by the transfer into a trust that meets the statute, for purposes of Florida law, as section 736.1502 defines it.

For community property held in a revocable trust, section 736.0602(2) permits either spouse to revoke and requires both spouses to amend. That mechanic is in amending or restating a revocable trust. A joint revocable trust that does not meet section 736.1503 is a common-law trust. Titling an account in both names does not produce the section 1014(b)(6) adjustment.
