Cross-Border, International & Wealth Structuring

A Foreign Grantor Trust That Becomes a Wyoming Dynasty Trust

By Rafael Recalde — November 22, 2026

Recalde Law Firm, P.A. is a Florida law firm with an office in Miami, Florida. We advise on United States law only. We do not practice Mexican law, and we do not give Mexican tax or succession advice. We do not practice the law of any other country. Formation of a non-U.S. entity, and the local law of that jurisdiction, are matters for local counsel. We handle the U.S. architecture: classification, the documents a U.S. custodian and a U.S. court will read, and coordination with the family's tax adviser.

The structure advisers ask for, once they have seen both halves of a Latin American family, is specific. The parent lives in Mexico, or elsewhere outside the United States, and wants to keep control. One or more children live in the United States, or will. The family wants a trust that can last for those children without becoming, at the parent's death, a foreign non-grantor trust that accumulates income for them. A foreign grantor trust during the parent's life, which becomes a domestic trust at death, is the shape that matches those facts. Wyoming is a common place to seat the domestic half. The family does not move there.

This is a U.S. classification note for the RIA, the private banker, and the family office. Mexican tax on the parent, on the funding, and on the death is for Mexican counsel. We do not give that advice. The U.S. person's basic exclusion is $15 million for 2026, indexed afterward. That figure belongs to a U.S. citizen or a U.S. domiciliary. It is not the parent's U.S. estate-tax computation.

While the parent is alive

Section 672(f) generally keeps a foreign person from being the income-tax owner of a trust. Two exceptions do the work in this design. The parent can have a power to revest the trust property in himself or herself. The power can be exercisable alone, or with the consent of a related or subordinate party who is subservient to the grantor. Treasury Regulation 1.672(f)-3(a) requires that the power be exercisable for at least 183 days of the year. Or, during the parent's life, the trust can distribute only to the parent or the parent's spouse. The deed has to meet one of those exceptions in fact. A letter of wishes does not. The comparison of the two trust types is in FGT versus FNGT.

If the second exception is the one the deed uses, a power in a U.S. child to receive distributions during the parent's life closes it. If the first exception is the one the deed uses, the parent alone can revoke, and the instrument can still name the children as the people who take after death. A distribution to a U.S. child during the parent's life, while grantor status holds, is generally a gift from the foreign parent, reported on Form 3520 when it crosses the foreign-gift threshold. It is not a distribution of accumulated trust income. The reporting contrast is in the Spanish note on distributions and in U.S. beneficiaries of foreign trusts.

The trust stays foreign during this period on purpose. A trust is domestic only if both tests in section 7701 are met: a U.S. court can exercise primary supervision, and U.S. persons control every substantial decision. A parent in Mexico who can revoke, or a protector in Mexico whose consent is required for a distribution, keeps a substantial decision outside U.S. persons. That is what the family means by control. It is also what keeps the trust foreign. The account title, the mandate, and the KYC file should name that person, so the custodian's records match the deed.

What has to be true on the day of death

The revocation power ends when the parent dies. If nothing else changes, and the trust is still foreign, and no other person is an owner under sections 671 through 679, the trust becomes a foreign non-grantor trust. Income that stays in it can become undistributed net income, and a later distribution to a U.S. child can carry the throwback computation. That sequence is the subject of when a parent's FGT becomes an FNGT, and the computation itself is in the throwback rule.

The domestic half of this design is a set of clauses that take effect at death, plus people who can actually serve. A U.S. trustee takes office. Every substantial decision, including distributions, investments, and the power to remove that trustee, sits with U.S. persons. No non-U.S. protector keeps a veto. A court in the United States can supervise the trust. When both section 7701 tests are met, the trust is domestic. Income earned after that point is taxed as domestic-trust income: to the trust if it is accumulated, or to the beneficiary as current distributable net income if it is carried out. Domestication does not take the trust out of the throwback rules. Section 665(c)(2)(A) keeps them for a domestic trust that was ever foreign. What it changes is who the trustee is and how post-death income is reported, so the practical control is to carry out each year's DNI currently and keep UNI from building.

Wyoming is a practical seat for that trustee. The state allows a long trust term when section 34-1-139 and its conditions are met, including a clause that itself requires interests and powers to vest or end within the period, and it has a directed-trust statute so an investment adviser and a distribution adviser can be different people from the administrative trustee. The family can live in Mexico City or in Miami. Those points are in a Wyoming trust when the family lives elsewhere. A Florida trust can also be domestic. The choice of state does not replace the two tests.

The gap, and the estate-tax file

A sentence that says the trust "becomes Wyoming" at death does not meet the tests if the successor has not accepted, if a Mexican protector must still approve distributions, or if the bank will not take instructions from the new trustee. Income earned in that gap, while the trust is an FNGT, can be undistributed net income. Domestication later does not erase that pool. The adviser wants the successor named, the decisions listed, and the custodian's onboarding started while the parent is alive.

Estate tax is a different statute from income-tax classification. A power to revoke, which is often the section 672(f) path, can include U.S.-situs assets in the parent's gross estate under sections 2036 and 2038. Foreign-situs property of a nonresident who is not a U.S. citizen is generally outside that gross estate. The credit under section 2102(b) is the limited exemption for the U.S.-situs portion. Section 2104(b) treats property transferred in a transfer described in sections 2035 through 2038 as U.S.-situs if the property was in the United States either at the transfer or at death. Retitling the asset, or moving it, after it has been funded into a revocable trust does not cure that situs. Making the trust domestic at death does not, by itself, pull foreign assets into the U.S. estate, and it does not remove U.S.-situs assets that the retained power already reached. The inventory is in U.S.-situs assets inside a foreign trust.

What belongs in the adviser's file

Before anyone calls this a dynasty trust, the file should show five things. Which section 672(f) exception the deed uses. Whether a U.S. child can receive anything while the parent is alive. Who holds substantial decisions today, and who holds them the day after death. That the successor is a U.S. person who has agreed to serve, with no non-U.S. veto left behind. And that Mexican counsel has the same diagram for Mexican tax. The U.S. work is the deed, the trustee, and the classification. It sits on Foreign Trusts and Private Wealth.
